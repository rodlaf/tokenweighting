\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning has become a standard ingredient in post-training large language models for alignment and reasoning. In reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR), a policy samples a completion, a verifier produces a scalar reward, and policy-gradient updates push probability mass toward rewarded trajectories. The difficulty is that the reward is sequence-level while the optimization is token-level. Every gradient step therefore makes an implicit decision about \emph{which tokens should absorb the credit}. In practice, most critic-free methods still broadcast the same scalar advantage to every token in the sampled completion.

That broadcast rule is convenient, but it is also arbitrary. Reasoning traces contain branch points, bookkeeping tokens, stylistic filler, and deterministic continuations. Treating all positions identically ignores the fact that some decisions are more informative for policy improvement than others. This observation motivates a growing literature on token-level reward densification, process supervision, token selection, and tree-based credit assignment for language RL and preference optimization. Our goal in this paper is narrower: we ask how much mileage one can get from \emph{intrinsic} signals already produced by the policy, without training additional critics or reward models.

The obvious reference point is value-based credit assignment. Classical actor--critic methods estimate a value function over states or prefixes and turn delayed rewards into per-step advantages. For language generation, however, prefixes are high-dimensional, non-stationary, and semantically heterogeneous. A prefix can be similar at the token level while implying very different future outcomes. This does not make value functions impossible, but it does make them brittle and expensive to estimate. Recent RLVR work has therefore shifted toward critic-free estimators such as RLOO and GRPO, which remove the value network entirely and use prompt-level baselines instead \citep{ahmadian2024backtobasics, shao2024deepseekmath}. Once the critic is removed, the next unresolved design choice is how to distribute a sequence-level learning signal inside the trajectory.

We study a simple answer: weight token-level policy gradients using quantities already available from the policy's predictive distribution. We focus on two intrinsic signals. The first is \emph{surprisal}, which emphasizes low-probability sampled actions. The second is \emph{entropy reduction}, which emphasizes tokens after which the next-token distribution becomes substantially more concentrated. Both signals are cheap, local, and model-internal. They require no token-level labels, no verifier decomposition, no prefix trees, and no auxiliary reward estimator.

Our contribution is deliberately scoped to a defensible novelty boundary. We are \emph{not} the first to study token-level credit assignment in language RL, the first to use confidence or entropy as a token salience signal, or the first to densify sequence-level rewards. Recent neighboring work already studies confidence-based hard token selection in preference optimization \citep{yoon2025confpo} and entropy-based subset selection in RLVR \citep{wang2025beyond8020}. We instead isolate a different point in the design space: \emph{continuous intrinsic token weighting for online verifiable-reward RL, implemented as a drop-in modifier of standard critic-free policy gradients}. This places our method between hard token selection methods and heavier token-level credit-assignment systems based on reward redistribution, process reward models, or rollout trees.

To make that design point directly testable, we evaluate intrinsic weighting in a controlled benchmark suite rather than in a large opaque training stack. The benchmark contains two verifiable sequence-generation settings: arithmetic traces and executable program traces. Each trajectory contains both semantically important decisions and reward-irrelevant filler positions, which allows us to measure not only task success but also whether the weighting schemes move update mass toward the right decisions. The benchmark and all experiments are implemented in a compact NumPy codebase under the repository's \texttt{experiment/} directory.

The empirical picture is mixed but encouraging. Surprisal weighting improves arithmetic pass@4 under an RLOO objective, while entropy-reduction weighting yields substantially larger gains on the program-trace task under GRPO-style normalization. Across both tasks, intrinsic weighting increases the fraction of token mass assigned to semantically important positions relative to the uniform baseline. However, the improvements do \emph{not} come from a uniform reduction in our gradient-variance proxy; if anything, the better-performing weighting rules often increase weighted score variance while allocating that variance more selectively. We view this as a useful clarification: intrinsic token weighting behaves less like a generic variance-reduction device and more like a structured credit-allocation prior.

The rest of the paper develops this claim. \S\ref{sec:related-work} positions the method relative to RLVR, dense token-reward methods, and token-selection approaches. \S\ref{sec:methods} formulates intrinsic token weighting as a biased but modular modification of critic-free policy gradients. \S\ref{sec:analysis} discusses the bias--variance trade-off and why intrinsic salience may still be useful when prefix values are weak. \S\ref{sec:experiments} presents the controlled benchmark and quantitative results.
